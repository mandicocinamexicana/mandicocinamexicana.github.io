Origin story

Born in Puebla

Chile en Nogada comes from Puebla, a colonial city east of Mexico City famous for its convent kitchens and the nuns who cooked in them. Its story is part history, part legend, and completely Mexican.

In 1821, after more than ten years of war, Mexico won its independence from Spain. Agustín de Iturbide, commander of the Trigarante Army, signed the Treaty of Córdoba in Veracruz and set off for Mexico City, with a stop in Puebla along the way.

As the story goes, the Augustinian nuns of the Convent of Santa Mónica, well known for their cooking, were asked to prepare a feast in his honor. They wanted a dish that would look like the brand-new nation itself.

A flag on your plate

The nuns gathered whatever green, white and red ingredients they could find to honor the colors of the Trigarante Army. Iturbide would later choose those same three colors for the Mexican flag, and they still fly today.

Why it's special

A dish for many hands

Chile en Nogada is a celebration of Mexico, and it takes a lot of time and a lot of hands to make. In Mexico, families come together to prepare it, then gather around the table to enjoy their hard work together.

Historians will tell you cooks in Puebla were making stuffed chiles and walnut sauces long before 1821. In Mexico, nobody minds. The story is too good, and the dish is too beautiful.
